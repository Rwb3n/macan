## Findings by topic (strength in brackets)

**Multi-step vs single page.**
- GOV.UK starts with one thing per page, for mobile use and error recovery (C1). It is a guideline.
- NN/g warns that too many steps add their own cost and recommends grouping questions that are used together (C4).
- The A/B evidence is thin and vendor-authored:
  - Instapage's controlled test gave +21.4%, which its authors call "very small" (C8);
  - Venture Harbour's 743% is a before/after, not a test (C7).
- Strength: moderate for "split into short steps"; weak for any size of lift.

**Number of fields, question order, progressive disclosure.**
- Baymard's testing finds the number of fields matters more than the number of steps: about 8 is enough, against an average of 11.3 (C3, C11).
- Optional fields should hide behind a link (C12), and extra fields hurt more on mobile (C13).
- GOV.UK: ask the rule-out questions first and branch (C2).
- Strength: strong for e-commerce checkout; carried over to quote forms by inference.

**When to ask for contact details.**
- Several NN/g sources agree: give value before asking, and asking too much too soon makes users leave (C18, C20, C21). A prominent address form before any content read as a sign-up wall (C19).
- Payment details asked during browsing caused errors (C5).
- Account creation does better at the end (C14); 18% abandon over forced accounts (C28).
- An unexplained phone field causes false entries and abandonment (C9).
- Strength: moderate to strong and consistent. All of it is usability testing or surveys; no A/B test on a quote form was found.

**Showing a price before contact.**
- NN/g, three sources:
  - users leave for competitors when prices are hidden (C24);
  - a home-services site with no stated rate was ruled out in 35 seconds (C22);
  - when an exact price is impossible, show ranges or typical cases rather than a long quote form (C23, C25, C26).
- Unexpected extra costs are the top stated reason for abandoning (40%) (C27).
- Thumbtack shows estimated prices on some jobs (C47); this is a description with no outcome data.
- Strength: moderate to strong (qualitative usability work, consistent over 2006-2016). No marketplace A/B test was found.

**Trust signals.**
- Reviews: the strongest area.
  - Nearly everyone reads them (C30, C31), and 53% seek out the negative ones (C31).
  - Replying to negative reviews helps (C32).
  - Most of the lift comes from the first five reviews; 4.0 to 4.7 beats a perfect 5.0 (C33, C34).
  - On Airbnb, a host with no reviews is about 4 times less likely to be booked (C35). A double-blind review process raised review rates by 7% (C36, Airbnb's own test).
  - High reputation outweighs the pull to trust people like oneself (C37, a peer-reviewed experiment with 8,906 users).
- Vetting and detailed evaluations help providers with no track record (C39, a field experiment).
- Badges and seals raise how secure a page feels, mainly next to where they sit (C38).
- Gap: no study isolates guarantees, or "vetted" badges for trades, on the buyer side.
- Strength: strong for reviews; moderate for badges; nothing found on guarantees.

**Progress indicators.**
- Show the steps and the current step (C6), and let people save and come back (C15).
- Experiments and a meta-analysis of 32 experiments: a constant-speed bar does not reduce drop-off, and a bar that looks slow early increases it (C16, C17).
- Implication (an inference, not a finding): keep the step count small and front-load the easy questions, rather than relying on a "% complete" bar.
- Strength: strong, but the experiments are web surveys.

**Follow-up and response time.**
- GOV.UK: the confirmation page must say what happens next and when (C10).
- Users expect a time estimate before committing (C29).
- Replying within an hour makes a firm about 7 times as likely to qualify the lead, yet the average reply took 42 hours and 23% never replied (C40, C41; an HBR audit from 2011).
- "Uncertain waits are longer" is theory, marked as opinion (C42).
- Strength: moderate. The audit is old and US, and it measures the provider side.

**Marketplace-specific.**
- Thumbtack:
  - having 3 or more quotes goes with a good customer experience (C43);
  - pre-matching pros to requests more than doubled the share of requests that reach 3+ quotes (C44; a rollout, not a controlled test);
  - its flow is free text, then a few questions, then matched pros "and why each one is a good match" (C45).
- Angi says its free-text AI helper converts about 3 times as well, but users choose to use it, so this is not causal (C46).
- Upwork, Etsy, Checkatrade: no buyer-flow design or engineering post with results was found.
- Strength: weak to moderate. These are the platforms' own reports.

**Mobile.**
- One thing per page helps on mobile (C1).
- Unneeded fields cost more on mobile (C13).
- Use the right keyboard type for each field (C48); automatic address or postcode lookup does best (C49).
- Strength: strong (Baymard testing).

## Defects and caveats
- No rows were dropped: all 49 have a URL and a verbatim quote.
- Five quotes were checked only against Firecrawl's search excerpt of the page, not a full scrape: C18, C29, C42, C43 and C45. The excerpts are page text, but these are the first rows to re-check.
- The Maister PDF link returned a 404, so C42 is quoted from the article page's excerpt.
- HBR is paywalled, so C40 and C41 are quoted from the authors' copy on ResearchGate.
- Most dated evidence is e-commerce checkout or web surveys. Carrying it over to request-for-quote is an inference.
- Searched but not found: Upwork, Etsy or Checkatrade buyer-flow posts with results, and any study of guarantees as a trust signal.
